Abstract

Brain-computer interface decoders lose accuracy as the recorded signal drifts. A monitor could warn before control is lost, but no accepted test shows whether such a warning comes early enough or is right often enough. This project builds that test. On archived recordings from two implanted arrays, with 1,850 planted faults whose start times were sealed in advance, the monitor detected faults that counting activity misses, yet warned a median 20 s after decoding failed and gave 3.41 false alarms per hour against a budget of 0.1. A preregistered optical test bench, specified but not yet built, will measure both quantities where failure times are known.

Introduction

Nearly 5.4 million people in the United States live with paralysis [3]. For some, an implanted brain-computer interface restores control of a cursor by intent. Its decoder goes wrong as the recording drifts, so decoders often need daily recalibration, and the device cannot be used freely while that happens [2].

Alarms that cry wolf get switched off: nuisance alarms are the leading reason smoke alarms are disconnected [5]. Existing work corrects drift automatically [2, 6] or reports that a drift score correlates with performance [1]. No published lead time or false-alarm rate was found for such a monitor.

Purpose

To measure how early a decoder-health monitor warns, and how often it warns when nothing is wrong, on test beds where the moment of failure is known in advance.

Hypothesis / Engineering goal

Hypothesis. Measured against failure times fixed in advance, lead time and false-alarm rate will fall outside what a deployed system could use. Falsified if the median lead time is above zero and the 95% upper bound on false alarms is within 0.1 per hour.

Engineering goal. Build the monitor, the benchmark and the test bench that make both measurable. Success is a trustworthy measurement, not a claim that the monitor works.
