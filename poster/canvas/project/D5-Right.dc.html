Discussion

What the results mean

The monitor carries real information. It beats plain counting on exactly the faults counting cannot see, on both arrays (Fig. 11). It also does not mistake a change of task for a fault.

Also measured: a change of task is not mistaken for a fault (15.6× and 70× separation), and accuracy by session ranges 0.32 to 0.97, tracking that day's decoder error (ρ = −0.720).

A real signal is not yet a usable warning. It comes 20 s late and 34 times too often (Figs 12 to 14). The binding problem is not detection but silence: the risk score is never quiet during healthy use.

The cause is measured, not guessed. One session holds about one independent measurement, so neither a different threshold nor averaging rescues it (Fig. 16).

One question stays open. Deciding less often helps on one array and hurts on the other (Fig. 17). Two arrays cannot settle it.

Open until both are read together: whether the late warning belongs to brain recordings or to the method itself; how large the false-alarm rate is once there is enough fault-free recording to bound it; and whether deciding less often helps.

Limitations
  • A camera in a sealed box is not an implanted array. A false-alarm rate measured on the bench is not an estimate of a clinical rate.
  • Matching the decoding difficulty makes the problem comparably hard. It does not make the noise, the drift or the failure modes the same.
  • Two arrays, which disagree on several questions; one bench, one room, one family of monitor.
  • About 4 held-out fault-free hours cannot show a rate below 0.75 per hour. Reaching the 0.1 budget needs about 30 h.
Broader significance

The two questions, how early and how often wrong, are not specific to brain implants. They apply to any many-channel sensor that drifts and is watched by a statistic, and the bench is one instance of that class. What carries over is the test: failures whose start times are sealed in advance, and timing and false alarms reported together. No clinical benefit is claimed.

Conclusion

On archived recordings the monitor detects real faults, but it warns too late and too often to be relied on, and the cause is measured. The bench will show whether that belongs to brain recordings or to the method.
