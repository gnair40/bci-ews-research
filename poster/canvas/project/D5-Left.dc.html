Abstract

Brain-computer interface decoders lose accuracy as the recorded signal drifts. A monitor could warn before control is lost, but no accepted test shows whether such a warning comes early enough or is right often enough. This project builds that test. On archived recordings from two implanted arrays, with 1,850 planted faults whose start times were sealed in advance, the monitor detected faults that counting activity misses, yet warned a median 20 s after decoding failed and gave 3.41 false alarms per hour against a budget of 0.1. A preregistered optical test bench, specified but not yet built, will measure both quantities where failure times are known.

Purpose

To measure how early a decoder-health monitor warns, and how often it warns when nothing is wrong, on test beds where the moment of failure is known in advance.

Hypothesis / Engineering goal

Primary hypothesis. Measured against failure times fixed in advance, with enough fault-free recording to bound the rate, the monitor's lead time and false-alarm rate fall outside what a deployed system could use. Falsified if the median lead time is above zero and the 95% upper bound on false alarms is within 0.1 per hour, at the threshold that detects 80% of faults.

Secondary hypothesis. A monitor can reach the correlation with performance that the field reports as evidence (r above 0.7) while those two quantities say it is unusable. Inconclusive if no correlation measure reaches 0.70.

Engineering goal. Build the monitor, the benchmark and the test bench that make both quantities measurable. Success is a trustworthy measurement, whatever the numbers are. It is not a claim that the monitor works.

Predictions and sample sizes were frozen on 24 September 2026, before any bench data existed (commit f4dda04).

  • It is fitted on healthy windows only. It never sees the decoder output, the task, the performance number or a fault label.
  • It works in log space, so a uniform gain change becomes a shift and separates from a change in shape across channels.
  • A warning needs the risk score to stay above its threshold, not touch it once.
What is measured

Fig. 3. One archived episode (array T11, day 692, gain drift), chosen by rule as the first whose lead time equals the median. A: the fault begins, at a time sealed in advance. B: decoding fails, its error 10° above the healthy baseline. C: the monitor warns. Lead time is B minus C, so a negative value means the warning came too late. Detection delay, C minus A, is what the bench adds.

Computational validationcomplete

Archived recordings do not say when a failure began, so the failures were built: faults planted in real recordings at start times sealed before any detector existed, graded by a decoder that is never refitted.

1Archived recordings
two implanted arrays, T11 and T5; public and de-identified [1]. No participants were recruited.
2Planted faults
1,850 episodes (1,073 + 777): four fault types, three rates, three severities; start times sealed with SHA-256.
3Frozen reference decoder
ridge regression, fitted once: 54.6° error against 90.7° chance (T11), 52.8° against 84.9° (T5). Grades only.
4The monitor
sees only recorded activity; a risk score every 5 s from the last 30 s.
5Scoring harness
lead time and false alarms as a pair; five gates; test data read once.
