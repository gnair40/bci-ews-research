Decoder-Guard/ Six studies, 2–3 September 2026/ T11 & T5, public iBCI recordings

The Bad-Day Problem

We spent a day asking six questions about a system that watches a brain–computer interface for signs of failure. The fifth answer was bad news. The sixth was an attempt to prove the fifth wrong — it survived, but it cost us our explanation of why.

What changed

The monitor works well on days when the decoder is working, and fails on days when the decoder is already struggling — the days it exists for.

Every earlier negative result said “not sensitive enough yet”, which is the kind of problem more work fixes. This one says the failures line up with the hazard. A monitor whose mistakes are unrelated to the danger can be improved by making it sharper. A monitor whose mistakes arrive with the danger cannot. What we cannot say is why — see study 06.

The chain

These are numbered because they genuinely are a sequence: each question was chosen by what the one before it found. Nothing here was picked from a menu of analyses after seeing results — and the last one exists purely to attack the one before it.

01

How much healthy recording does it take to set one of these up?

About two minutes. Twenty healthy 30-second windows gets you everything the full 19-minute record gets you. More data adds nothing — on both arrays it makes things very slightly worse.

T11  20 windows 0.654 → all 221 0.648 T5  20 windows 0.740 → all 211 0.739

Why it saturates so fast. A session's healthy record barely varies within itself — consecutive windows correlate at r = 0.995. That is the same fact that wrecked detection power earlier in the project. Here it works in our favour: the 221st window has nothing to say that the 20th did not.

02

Once it is set up, how long does that setup stay good?

No measurable ageing over 142 days. Fitting the monitor on one session and using it five months later costs roughly 0.017 AUC — which is nothing.

slope −0.012 AUC / 100 days 95 forward day pairs, gaps 3–142 d permutation p = 0.128

The statistic that would have been wrong. The obvious test gives p = 0.003 and reads as a clear result. But those 95 day pairs come from only 15 sessions, so they share days constantly and are nowhere near independent. Shuffling which calendar date belongs to which session — keeping every measurement, destroying only the link to elapsed time — gives p = 0.128. The naive test understated by 43×.

03

If age does not matter, what does?

Which day you use it on — enormously. Across 13 sessions on T11 the monitor's accuracy runs from 0.32 to 0.97. Three days fall below chance, meaning its risk score ran backwards: lower during faults than during healthy use.

worst day 0.32 best day 0.97 only 26% is sampling noise I² 0.86

Why nobody had seen this. Every earlier analysis pooled all the sessions together into one number, and pooling averages this away. The spread across days is larger than the entire range any feature or detector change ever moved that pooled number.

04

So what makes a day good or bad?

How well the decoder itself is doing that day. Registered in advance: five candidate explanations, each with its predicted direction written down before anything was computed. Four came back null. The fifth is the finding.

ρ = −0.720, p = 0.0055 threshold p < 0.0125 (Bonferroni) T5 agrees: ρ = −0.829

It had to survive an obvious objection. The score is itself defined using decoder performance, so a bad-decoder day might depress it mechanically. Every link in that chain was tested and none exists — and controlling for it makes the relationship stronger, ρ = −0.773, p = 0.0032.

05

Then can the monitor at least tell you it is having a bad day?

No. Four signals computed from the decoder's own output — with no target, no intent, no labels of any kind — and not one of them predicts whether the monitor will work that day.

L1 speed variability — L2 reversal rate — L3 output autocorrelation — L4 mean speed —

The one that nearly worked, and why it is worse than a null. Mean output speed tracks decoder error on both arrays and clears the threshold on both — the strongest cross-participant agreement in the whole project. The signs are opposite: ρ = +0.681 on T11, ρ = −0.943 on T5. Any rule that abstains correctly on one array abstains backwards on the other.

06

Can we prove study 04 wrong?

The finding survived. The explanation did not. A fault only counts as damage if it pushes error 10° above that session's own baseline — and on a day already sitting at 87.5°, with chance at 90.7°, there is barely any room left. If faults simply do less on bad days, the whole result is about the ruler, not the monitor. I predicted, in writing, that this is what we would find.

ceiling is real: ρ = −0.747 but damage → accuracy +0.181, p = 0.55 04 controlling for damage: −0.794

Two of three predictions wrong, and then a retraction. Weaker faults are not what breaks the monitor — controlling for damage made study 04 stronger. But chasing a contradiction on day 783 (0.4° of damage, yet 78% of its episodes counted as deteriorating) turned up a second variable: how erratic the decoder's output is that day. It predicts accuracy at ρ = −0.720 — identical to study 04's number — and the two correlate at 0.813. See near-miss 04.

Whose fault is it?

Before calling this a flaw in our monitor, one more question: does every fault detector fail on the same days? I predicted yes — all four, correlating above 0.7 — and wrote that down first. I was wrong, and being wrong was more useful than being right.

Detector What it looks at Agrees with our monitor, day by day Shares the bad-day pattern?
distribution_shift the full multivariate distribution ρ = +0.835   p = 0.0004 Yes
robust_dispersion one number: spread ρ = +0.247   p = 0.42 No
mean_activity one number: total spikes ρ = −0.060   p = 0.84 No

The two that share it

Both model the shape of neural activity against a healthy reference.

On a day the decoder handles badly, the structure of the activity is unusual — which is exactly what a decoder is sensitive to, and exactly what this kind of monitor needs to stay stable. A monitor built on the same information the decoder uses inherits the decoder's bad days.

The two that do not

Both crush a window down to a single number.

They have no reference structure to lose, so bad days do not hurt them. They are also the detectors that cannot see most faults in the first place — which is where this whole project started.

The tension, unresolved

The property that makes a detector sensitive enough to be useful is the same property that makes it fail when the decoder fails.

Which means the objection is not to this build. It is to distribution-based decoder-health monitoring as a class. Rebuilding the monitor differently while keeping the same kind of healthy reference will not escape it.

Three things I got wrong, and how they were caught

Each of these would have become a published claim. They are recorded here for the same reason they are recorded in the research log: a result you cannot see the mistakes behind is not a result.

Near-miss 01

An AUC of 0.836 that does not exist

Averaging the per-day scores by their precision gave 0.836, against a plain average of 0.675. I was a paragraph from writing “the monitor is far better than every earlier report said.”

Caught by: AUC is bounded between 0 and 1, so its uncertainty shrinks near the edges — meaning precision-weighting silently over-weights the most extreme days. Measured: ρ = −0.913 between how extreme a day was and how small its error bar was, with a 31× spread in weights. The field is now named ..._BIASED_DO_NOT_QUOTE.

Near-miss 02

A control that measured nothing and looked like it passed

The check for whether some days simply drew easier faults compared a numeric field against the text "high". It was always false, so it reported 0.00 on every day — which reads exactly like a clean pass.

Caught by: noticing that every day reported precisely zero. Fixing it changed a conclusion: on T5 the day-to-day spread does track fault mix (ρ = 0.886), so T5 cannot corroborate the T11 finding after all.

Near-miss 03

Designing on a signal that does not exist at run time

I wrote that decoder error is “measurable online, without labels.” It is not. That error is measured against where the user intended to move, which is known here only because these are recorded task blocks with logged targets. In real use there is no target.

Caught by: trying to specify the abstention rule concretely and finding the input unavailable. The correction is written into the report rather than edited away, because it would otherwise have shaped a design.

Near-miss 04

Replacing one unfounded “because” with another

Having found that the day's noisiness predicts the monitor's accuracy exactly as well as the decoder's error does, I started writing “noise is the real explanation.” It would have read as a correction — a careful researcher refining their claim.

Caught by: running the partial correlation in both directions instead of one. Decoder error controlling for noise: ρ = −0.333, p = 0.29. Noise controlling for decoder error: ρ = −0.333, p = 0.29. Identical. That is collinearity, not one explaining the other — at 13 sessions, with the two predictors correlating at 0.813, this study simply cannot tell them apart. So the mechanism claim from study 04 is withdrawn rather than replaced.

Where this leaves the project

The problem was never that the monitor is not sensitive enough. Its reliability is governed by session-level properties it cannot observe, and its failures arrive together with the hazard it exists to detect.

That association has now withstood three separate attempts to explain it away, and it argues against the design as originally conceived. It is reported that way rather than softened.

What we cannot say is which property. “How badly the decoder is doing” and “how erratic its output is” are the same variable at 13 sessions. Separating them needs days where the decoder is bad but steady, or noisy but accurate — and this dataset may not contain any.

OpenAre there sessions where the decoder is bad but steady, or noisy but accurate? Without them the two explanations stay welded together.
